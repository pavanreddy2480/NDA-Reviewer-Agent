# Capital Expenditure Policy

**Keystone Precision Manufacturing, Inc.**
Policy owner: Chief Financial Officer
Policy approver: Board of Directors
Effective date: January 13, 2025
Review cycle: annually, in the fourth quarter budget cycle
Document number: KPM-FN-006

---

## 1. Purpose

1.1 Keystone Precision Manufacturing, Inc. ("Keystone") is a capital-intensive business. A five-axis machining center costs more than a million dollars, a vacuum furnace costs several times that, and a new building or a plant expansion commits tens of millions. Decisions to spend this money determine our capacity, our capabilities, our quality, our safety and our cost position for a decade or more. They also draw on the same cash that funds payroll, inventory and growth.

1.2 This policy sets out how Keystone plans, evaluates, approves, executes and reviews capital spending. Its goals are to direct capital to projects that create the most value; to ensure that spending is authorized at the right level; to control cost and schedule once a project is approved; to confirm afterward that the expected results were achieved; and to protect the Company's assets.

## 2. Scope and Definitions

2.1 **Scope.** This policy applies to all purchases, construction and improvements of fixed assets by Keystone and its subsidiaries. It applies to new assets, replacement of existing assets, expansion, upgrades, capitalized software and leasehold improvements. Operating expenses, inventory purchases and routine maintenance and repair are governed by the Finance Procedures Manual and the Supplier Engagement Policy.

2.2 **Definitions.**

- **Capital expenditure (capex):** spending that creates or extends the useful life of an asset with a cost of $10,000 or more and a useful life of more than one year, recorded on the balance sheet and depreciated.
- **Project:** a set of related capital expenditures that serve a single objective and are managed together.
- **Maintenance capex:** spending to replace or restore existing assets in order to sustain current operations.
- **Growth capex:** spending to add capacity or capability, to enter new markets or to reduce cost.
- **Compliance capex:** spending required by law, regulation or a customer requirement, including safety and environmental projects.
- **Capital Appropriation Request (CAR):** the standard form used to request approval of a project.
- **Hurdle rate:** the minimum acceptable rate of return, set by the Chief Financial Officer; currently 14 percent after tax for growth projects and 10 percent for cost reduction projects.
- **Payback period:** the time required to recover the initial investment from the project's cash flows.
- **Net present value (NPV):** the present value of expected cash flows, discounted at the Company's cost of capital, less the initial investment.
- **Internal rate of return (IRR):** the discount rate at which the NPV of a project equals zero.

## 3. Capital Planning and Budgeting

3.1 **Annual plan.** Each fall, plant managers and functional leaders submit a capital request list for the following year, together with a three-year outlook. Requests are classified as maintenance, growth or compliance, ranked by priority and supported by a preliminary business case.

3.2 **Prioritization.** The Capital Review Committee, composed of the Chief Financial Officer, the Chief Operating Officer, the Chief Technology Officer and the Vice President, Engineering, ranks the requests using the following criteria in this order: safety and legal compliance; customer commitments and capacity constraints; return on investment; strategic fit with the technology roadmap; and ability to execute.

3.3 **Budget.** The Board of Directors approves the annual capital budget in December. The budget is allocated into categories: maintenance, growth, compliance and a contingency pool. The contingency pool is limited to 5 percent of the total budget and is released only by the Chief Financial Officer.

3.4 **Unbudgeted spending.** Projects that were not included in the approved budget may be considered during the year if they respond to an urgent safety, compliance or customer need. They require approval at the level that would be required for their size, and in addition the Chief Financial Officer must confirm that funding is available from contingency or from the deferral of another project.

## 4. Evaluation of Projects

4.1 **Business case.** A CAR must include the objective and scope of the project; alternatives considered, including doing nothing and buying used equipment; total cost including installation, tooling, training, software, utilities and facility modifications; expected benefits, quantified wherever possible; a timeline; risks; and effects on safety, environment and quality.

4.2 **Financial analysis.** Growth projects and cost reduction projects must show NPV, IRR and payback over a period equal to the useful life of the asset, up to a maximum of ten years. The base case uses conservative volume and price assumptions agreed with Finance. A sensitivity analysis shows the effect of a 20 percent decline in volume and a 10 percent increase in cost. Projects for which the IRR is below the hurdle rate are approved only if justified by strategic, safety or compliance factors.

4.3 **Maintenance and compliance projects.** For these projects, the CAR must document the consequence of not proceeding, the lowest-cost option that satisfies the need, and a comparison of repair and replacement costs. Financial return is not required for compliance projects but cost-effectiveness is.

4.4 **Lease versus buy.** For equipment that may be leased, Finance analyzes the lease-versus-buy decision, considering cost of capital, tax effects, residual value, technology risk and flexibility. A lease with total payments above $250,000 is reviewed by the Chief Financial Officer as if it were a purchase.

4.5 **Technology and obsolescence.** Machine tool and controls technology move fast. The Vice President, Engineering confirms that the proposed equipment is compatible with Keystone's programming systems and cell standards, that spare parts and service will be available for at least ten years and that the supplier is financially stable.

4.6 **Post-audit.** The business case author reports results against forecast twelve months after a project is complete and again at twenty-four months. Finance summarizes variances for the Capital Review Committee. Systematic over-optimism by a function is taken into account in the future review of its requests.

## 5. Approval Authority

5.1 **Approval levels.** The total project cost, including contingency, determines the level of authority required.

| Total project cost | Approval required |
|---|---|
| Under $25,000 | Plant manager or department head |
| $25,000 to under $100,000 | Plant manager and plant controller |
| $100,000 to under $500,000 | Vice President of the sponsoring function and Chief Financial Officer |
| $500,000 to under $2,000,000 | Chief Operating Officer and Chief Financial Officer |
| $2,000,000 to under $5,000,000 | Chief Executive Officer, on recommendation of the Capital Review Committee |
| $5,000,000 and above | Board of Directors, on recommendation of the Chief Executive Officer |

5.2 **Aggregation.** Related spending is aggregated to determine the approval level. A project may not be split into smaller parts to avoid review. Spending on a project in excess of the approved amount must be reapproved if it exceeds the lesser of 10 percent or $250,000.

5.3 **Approval sequence.** The CAR is signed by the sponsor, reviewed by Finance, and approved at the relevant level. No order may be placed, no work may begin and no commitment may be made before the CAR is fully approved, except for emergency purchases described in section 5.4.

5.4 **Emergency purchases.** If a failure threatens safety, environmental compliance or the ability to deliver to a customer, a plant manager may authorize emergency spending up to $100,000, and the Chief Operating Officer up to $500,000, with a CAR submitted within five business days. The Chief Financial Officer is notified within one business day of the commitment.

## 6. Project Execution and Control

6.1 **Project management.** Each approved project has a named project manager, a schedule, a budget, a risk register and a reporting plan. For projects above $500,000, the project manager reports monthly on progress, cost to date, forecast to complete and risks.

6.2 **Procurement.** Equipment and construction are procured according to the Supplier Engagement Policy. The sponsor and the buyer document the evaluation of alternatives. For purchases above $500,000, a written specification is issued to at least three qualified bidders unless the Vice President, Supply Chain approves a single-source purchase.

6.3 **Installation, qualification and acceptance.** Equipment is installed according to manufacturer requirements and Keystone standards. Before production use, equipment undergoes a qualification: installation checks, operational checks, accuracy and repeatability tests, and a trial run. For special processes, additional qualification is required by the Quality Management Policy. The sponsor accepts the equipment only when qualification is complete and training has been delivered.

6.4 **Safety review.** Environmental Health and Safety reviews each project before approval to identify hazards and permits, and again before start-up to verify that guarding, lockout points, ventilation and emergency stops are in place.

6.5 **Change control.** Changes in scope, cost or schedule are documented. Cost increases above the thresholds in section 5.2 must be reapproved at the level corresponding to the new total.

6.6 **Capitalization and closeout.** When the asset is ready for its intended use, Finance moves the cost from construction in progress to fixed assets and begins depreciation. The project manager completes a closeout report, including final cost, lessons learned and open items, within sixty days of acceptance.

## 7. Asset Management

7.1 **Asset register.** Finance maintains the fixed asset register, including a unique tag for each asset, its location, cost, date placed in service, useful life, depreciation method and responsible manager. Tags are physically attached to assets.

7.2 **Physical verification.** Plant controllers perform a full physical inventory of fixed assets once a year and report differences to the Chief Financial Officer. Missing assets are investigated.

7.3 **Transfers and disposals.** Assets that move between plants or are no longer needed are reported to Finance. Sales or scrapping of assets with a book value above $25,000 requires the approval of the Vice President of the sponsoring function and the Chief Financial Officer. Proceeds are recorded in the correct account, and disposals of equipment are coordinated with Environmental Health and Safety for removal of fluids and hazardous components.

7.4 **Depreciation.** Machinery and equipment are depreciated on a straight-line basis over seven to twelve years, depending on the asset class. Buildings are depreciated over thirty-nine years, leasehold improvements over the shorter of the lease or useful life, and capitalized software over three to five years. Changes in estimates are approved by the Chief Financial Officer and reviewed with the external auditors.

7.5 **Impairment.** The Controller assesses assets annually for indicators of impairment, such as a permanent decline in demand, technical obsolescence or damage. Impairment is recognized in accordance with accounting standards and reported to the Audit Committee.

7.6 **Insurance.** The Risk Manager ensures that assets are insured at replacement cost, and that the insurer is told of new high-value assets within thirty days of acceptance.

## 8. Government Incentives and Financing

8.1 Finance and the Legal Department look for tax incentives, grants and financing programs that may reduce the cost of a project, including bonus depreciation, state investment credits, workforce training grants, and energy efficiency incentives. Applications are prepared and signed by Finance after the project is approved.

8.2 Projects financed by debt or involving sale and leaseback require the approval of the Chief Financial Officer and, above $5,000,000, the Board. Compliance with covenants under the Company's credit facility is verified by the Treasurer before approval.

## 9. Roles and Responsibilities

9.1 **Board of Directors.** Approves the annual capital budget and projects of $5,000,000 or more.

9.2 **Chief Financial Officer.** Owns this policy, sets hurdle rates, chairs the Capital Review Committee, monitors spending against budget and reports quarterly to the Board.

9.3 **Capital Review Committee.** Reviews requests, ranks them and makes recommendations.

9.4 **Sponsors.** Prepare CARs, are accountable for the business case, and deliver the benefits.

9.5 **Project managers.** Deliver the project to schedule, cost and quality.

9.6 **Finance and Plant Controllers.** Analyze cases, track costs, maintain the asset register and perform the post-audit.

9.7 **Environmental Health and Safety, Quality and Engineering.** Review projects for their areas and confirm readiness before start-up.

9.8 **Internal Audit.** Tests compliance with this policy yearly, including a review of approvals and splitting.

## 10. Exceptions

10.1 Exceptions to this policy are granted only by the Chief Financial Officer and, for projects of $2,000,000 or more, by the Chief Executive Officer, in writing and with an explanation of the reason. They are reported to the Audit Committee each quarter.

## 11. Enforcement

11.1 Commitments made without the required approval, splitting of projects, and falsified business cases are serious violations of the Code of Business Conduct and may result in disciplinary action, up to and including termination.

11.2 Managers who approve spending outside their authority are accountable for the result, and amounts committed without authority may be charged to the manager's budget.

## Appendix A. CAR Checklist and Worked Example

A.1 **CAR checklist.** Before a Capital Appropriation Request is submitted, the sponsor confirms that each item below is complete: project title, plant, sponsor and project manager are stated; the category (maintenance, growth or compliance) is marked; the objective and scope are described in one page or less; alternatives, including used equipment, leasing and doing nothing, are listed with costs; total cost estimates are supported by at least one written quotation; installation, tooling, fixtures, programming, training, rigging, electrical and utility connections and facility changes are included; contingency is shown (typically 10 percent for equipment and 15 percent for construction); the financial analysis is attached and reviewed by Finance; the EHS review is attached; schedule and key milestones are provided; risks are listed with mitigation; and required permits are identified.

A.2 **Worked example: a new five-axis machining center.** The Toledo plant requests a five-axis machining center to take on a family of titanium structural brackets for which three customers have indicated volume commitments. The equipment quotation is $1,350,000. Installation and rigging add $60,000, tooling and fixtures $210,000, programming and probing software $45,000, training $20,000 and an electrical upgrade $85,000, for a subtotal of $1,770,000. A 10 percent contingency of $177,000 brings the total project cost to $1,947,000, which falls in the band of $500,000 to under $2,000,000, requiring the approval of the Chief Operating Officer and the Chief Financial Officer. The contingency brings the project close to the next threshold, and the sponsor is advised that an overrun above $53,000 would move the project to the Chief Executive Officer.

The financial case assumes incremental revenue of $2,600,000 per year at full volume, reached over eighteen months, a contribution margin of 34 percent after material and direct labor, and incremental fixed costs for maintenance and floor space of $110,000 per year. Annual after-tax cash flow at full volume is about $560,000 before depreciation tax shield effects. Over a ten-year life, the project yields an IRR of 19 percent and a payback of 3.9 years, above the 14 percent hurdle rate for growth projects. The sensitivity analysis shows that with volume down 20 percent and costs up 10 percent, IRR falls to 9 percent, which is below the hurdle rate. The Capital Review Committee therefore asks the sponsor to obtain signed volume commitments from at least two of the three customers before the purchase order is placed. The project is approved subject to that condition.

A.3 **Worked example: emergency replacement.** A spindle failure on a horizontal machining center at Dayton halts the only machine qualified for a flange used on a medical customer's line, and the customer has only one week of safety stock. The repair quote is $180,000 and the lead time is eight weeks, while a refurbished spindle assembly that can be installed in two weeks costs $155,000. The plant manager cannot authorize the $155,000 refurbished assembly alone, because it exceeds the plant manager's emergency limit of $100,000 under section 5.4, so the Chief Operating Officer approves it the same day under the $500,000 emergency limit. The Chief Financial Officer is notified within one business day, and the sponsor files a CAR within five business days. Because the failed spindle is covered under a service plan, Finance also pursues a partial credit.

A.4 **Capital spending calendar.** September: plants submit capital request lists. October: the Capital Review Committee meets for ranking sessions at each plant. November: preliminary budget is assembled and reviewed by the Chief Executive Officer. December: the Board approves the annual capital budget. January: approved projects are released for CAR preparation. Quarterly: Finance reports spending against budget, committed and uncommitted amounts, and forecasts for the year. July: mid-year review of the plan and reprioritization where conditions have changed.

A.5 **Common reasons CARs are returned.** Approvers report that the following problems cause most delays: quotations that do not cover installation and tooling; benefits stated without a baseline; a volume forecast that differs from the sales plan; absence of an EHS review; missing alternatives; contingency that is not shown; and a CAR that is submitted after a purchase order has been placed. Sponsors are encouraged to meet with their plant controller at least two weeks before they expect to submit.
