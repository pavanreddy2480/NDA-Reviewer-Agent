# Trade Secret Protection Policy

**Keystone Precision Manufacturing, Inc.**
Policy owner: Chief Technology Officer
Policy approver: Executive Leadership Team
Effective date: March 1, 2025
Review cycle: every two years, or sooner after a material change in law or in our products
Document number: KPM-IP-001

---

## 1. Purpose

1.1 Keystone Precision Manufacturing, Inc. ("Keystone" or "the Company") designs and builds precision-machined components for aerospace, medical device, energy and heavy-equipment customers. What separates Keystone from competitors with similar machines is not the machines themselves. It is how we program them, how we fixture and tool a part, how we sequence heat treatment and finishing, how we hold a tolerance over a long production run, and how we have learned to avoid the failures that other shops repeat. Much of that know-how is not patented and cannot be patented. It stays valuable only as long as it stays secret.

1.2 This policy explains what Keystone treats as a trade secret, who is responsible for protecting it, how it is classified and handled inside our plants and offices, and what we require whenever a trade secret has to leave the building for a legitimate business reason such as a supplier qualification, a customer design review or a distributor training session.

1.3 The policy is meant to do three things. First, it helps us meet the legal definition of a trade secret under the federal Defend Trade Secrets Act and the Ohio Uniform Trade Secrets Act, both of which require the owner to take reasonable measures to keep information secret. Second, it gives engineers, buyers, sales staff and managers a simple set of habits that are easy to follow on a busy production day. Third, it creates a record we can show a court, an auditor or a customer to demonstrate that our protective measures are real and consistently applied.

## 2. Scope and Definitions

2.1 **Scope.** This policy applies to every Keystone employee, officer, temporary worker, intern and individual contractor, at every Keystone facility including the Dayton headquarters, the Toledo and Erie machining plants, the Monterrey, Mexico assembly cell and any remote or home workspace. It applies to information in every form: paper, electronic files, models and drawings, programs for numerically controlled machines, physical samples and tooling, photographs, and knowledge carried in people's heads.

2.2 **Definitions.** The following terms have these meanings in this policy.

- **Trade secret:** information that derives independent economic value from not being generally known or readily ascertainable, and that Keystone takes reasonable measures to keep secret. Trade secrets include, without limitation, formulas, process parameters, programs, designs, patterns, compilations, methods, techniques and tooling configurations.
- **Tier 1 material:** the small set of trade secrets whose loss would materially damage Keystone's competitive position. Tier 1 material is listed by name in the Trade Secret Register.
- **Tier 2 material:** proprietary technical information that is valuable but whose loss would be serious rather than critical. Examples include standard work instructions, non-critical fixture designs, supplier scorecards and routine inspection plans.
- **Tier 3 material:** internal information that is not technical and does not need special handling beyond ordinary good practice.
- **Information owner:** the manager who is accountable for a body of information, normally the head of the function that created it.
- **Trade Secret Register:** the controlled list, maintained by the Intellectual Property Counsel, that identifies Tier 1 material, its information owner, its storage location and the people who have approved access.
- **Outside party:** any person or organization that is not a Keystone employee, including suppliers, distributors, customers, consultants, auditors, regulators and visitors.

2.3 **What we consider a trade secret.** Without being exhaustive, Keystone regards the following categories as potentially protectable: machining strategies for difficult alloys such as Inconel and titanium; cutter paths and feeds-and-speeds libraries tuned over many years; fixture and workholding designs; heat treatment cycles and furnace load patterns; surface finishing recipes; gauge designs and measurement routines; scrap and yield data by part family; cost models; and customer-specific design packages that a customer has entrusted to us.

2.4 **What is not a trade secret.** Information that is publicly available, that is part of the general skill and experience of a machinist or engineer, or that appears in a published patent or standard is not a Keystone trade secret even if it appears inside a Keystone document. Employees should not label information as secret when it is not, because over-labeling weakens the credibility of the real protections.

## 3. Terms Required Whenever Trade Secrets Leave Keystone

3.1 **Spoken information.** A great deal of Keystone's most valuable know-how is shared in conversation: at a first-article inspection, during a plant walk with a supplier's engineers, over a video call with a customer's design team or at a whiteboard during a design review. Our position is that knowledge conveyed aloud deserves the same protection as a drawing or a file, and any written nondisclosure agreement that Keystone signs must state that information communicated verbally counts as protected information just as written material does.

3.2 **Use only for the job at hand.** Information that Keystone releases to an outside party is released for one reason. Every nondisclosure agreement we sign must therefore restrict the recipient to using what it learns solely for the specific purposes described in that agreement, and for nothing else, including its own product development, quoting to other customers or training of its own staff.

3.3 **Logging external disclosures.** Whenever Tier 1 material is shown or sent to an outside party, the information owner must record the disclosure in the Trade Secret Register within five business days. The entry names the recipient, the date, the material disclosed, the business reason and the Keystone employee who made the disclosure. The Intellectual Property Counsel reviews the Register each quarter and follows up on entries that appear unusual, such as repeated disclosure of the same Tier 1 item to several unrelated organizations. Disclosure of Tier 2 material does not need to be logged individually but should be noted in the project file.

3.4 **No taking apart our hardware.** Keystone frequently provides first-article samples, prototype parts, fixtures, gauges and software builds to outside parties for evaluation. The physical object can reveal a process just as a drawing can. We require that any nondisclosure agreement forbid the recipient from analyzing our samples, prototypes, tooling or software in order to work out how they were made, including disassembly, decompilation, chemical or metallurgical analysis, and dimensional scanning intended to recreate them.

3.5 **Visitors and plant tours.** Visitors to a Keystone plant must be registered at reception, wear a visitor badge, and be escorted at all times. No photography or recording is permitted on the shop floor without the written permission of the plant manager. The host must confirm before the tour that Tier 1 equipment, work in progress and screens displaying Tier 1 material have been covered or turned off. Areas that hold Tier 1 processes are marked with a red floor border and are closed to visitors unless the Chief Technology Officer has given specific written permission.

## 4. Ownership and Rights

4.1 **No transfer of rights.** When Keystone shares information with an outside party, it does not give up ownership, and it does not permit the recipient to use the information as though it were the recipient's own. Each nondisclosure agreement we sign must make clear that the other side acquires no license and no other right of any kind in our information, designs or processes by virtue of receiving them.

4.2 **Inventions and improvements.** Employees assign to Keystone all inventions, improvements and works of authorship that they create within the scope of their work, as set out in the employee inventions assignment that each employee signs on joining. The Intellectual Property Counsel decides whether an invention should be patented, kept as a trade secret or published defensively. As a general rule, a process improvement that would be difficult for a competitor to detect from a finished part is kept as a trade secret, while a feature visible on the finished part is considered for patent protection.

4.3 **Third-party information.** Keystone also holds the trade secrets of its customers and suppliers. Employees must not bring to Keystone, and must not use at Keystone, confidential information belonging to a former employer or to anyone else without that party's authorization. Managers who hire from competitors must remind new hires of this obligation in writing during onboarding.

4.4 **Open source and publications.** Technical staff who wish to publish a paper, give a conference talk or contribute to an open-source project must submit the proposed material to the Intellectual Property Counsel at least fifteen business days in advance. The Counsel will confirm that no Tier 1 or Tier 2 material is disclosed and will register any invention that should be filed first.

## 5. Duration and Continuity of Protection

5.1 **Protection that outlasts the relationship.** The business relationship with an outside party will usually end long before the information we shared has lost its value. For that reason each nondisclosure agreement must state that the recipient's duties of secrecy and the other terms that are meant to continue do not fall away when the agreement is terminated or expires, but remain in force afterward.

5.2 **Reviewing the register.** At least once a year, each information owner reviews the entries in the Trade Secret Register for their function and confirms that each item still qualifies as Tier 1, that the access list is current, and that storage controls are still working. Items that have been overtaken by technology, published by others or abandoned are downgraded or removed, and the change is recorded with the reason.

5.3 **Minimum length of the secrecy period.** Our experience is that the know-how we protect typically remains competitively useful for at least five years after we share it. Every nondisclosure agreement Keystone signs must therefore keep the recipient's secrecy duties running for a period of not less than 60 months. The Legal Review and Approval Policy sets the maximum period and the route for any exception.

5.4 **Minimum survival after termination.** Where the agreement ends before the secrecy period would otherwise run out, the recipient's duties must continue after termination for at least 36 months. The Legal Review and Approval Policy sets the upper limit for this period.

5.5 **Information that remains a trade secret.** Nothing in section 5.3 or 5.4 limits Keystone's rights under trade secret law. Where the law allows information to be protected for as long as it remains secret, Keystone relies on that protection regardless of the duration chosen for any individual agreement.

## 6. Classification and Handling Inside Keystone

6.1 **Marking.** Every document, drawing, model and programme that contains Tier 1 or Tier 2 material carries a header or title-block legend: "Keystone Proprietary - Tier 1" or "Keystone Proprietary - Tier 2." Marking is a convenience for employees and a piece of evidence of our reasonable measures. A missing mark never means that the information is unprotected.

6.2 **Storage.** Tier 1 material in electronic form is stored only in the engineering vault on the product lifecycle management system, with access limited to the named people on the Trade Secret Register. Tier 1 paper documents are kept in locked cabinets and are signed out through the document control office. Tier 1 material must not be stored on personal devices, personal cloud accounts, removable drives that have not been issued by Keystone, or in consumer messaging applications.

6.3 **Printing and removal.** Tier 1 material may be printed only on the secure print queue, collected immediately and returned to document control or shredded after use. Employees may not remove Tier 1 material from a Keystone facility without the written approval of the information owner, and the approval must identify the material, the destination and the date of return.

6.4 **Need to know.** Access to Tier 1 material is granted on a need-to-know basis. A request for access is made through the service desk, approved by the information owner and logged. Access is reviewed every six months and removed when a person changes role. When an employee leaves, access is removed on the last day of employment, and the departing employee's manager completes the exit checklist, which includes returning badges, laptops, notebooks, samples and any paper records.

6.5 **Remote work.** Employees who work from home may access Tier 2 material through the company virtual private network on a Keystone-issued laptop. Tier 1 material may be viewed remotely only through the virtual desktop, which prevents downloading, printing and screen capture. Employees working remotely must not allow family members or visitors to see their screens and should lock their devices whenever they step away.

6.6 **Disposal.** Documents containing Tier 1 or Tier 2 material are shredded by cross-cut shredder or placed in the locked consoles collected by the Company's certified shredding vendor. Electronic media are wiped or physically destroyed according to the Information Security Standard. Scrap parts that carry a Tier 1 marking or that reveal a Tier 1 process are scrapped under the supervision of the plant quality manager.

## 7. Roles and Responsibilities

7.1 **Chief Technology Officer.** Owns this policy, decides which items are listed as Tier 1, approves exceptions to the handling rules in section 6 and reports annually to the Executive Leadership Team on the state of trade secret protection.

7.2 **Intellectual Property Counsel.** Maintains the Trade Secret Register, advises on whether information qualifies for protection, reviews the quarterly disclosure log, coordinates with outside patent counsel and leads any investigation of suspected misappropriation.

7.3 **Information owners.** Identify and classify the information their function creates, approve access, record external disclosures, and carry out the annual review of Register entries.

7.4 **Plant managers.** Ensure that visitor controls, area markings, shredding practices and physical security are in place and working at their facility.

7.5 **Managers.** Ensure that their teams complete training, follow the handling rules and report incidents promptly. Managers are expected to raise any concern about a colleague's handling of Tier 1 material with the Intellectual Property Counsel rather than ignore it.

7.6 **All personnel.** Follow this policy, complete the annual trade secret awareness module within thirty days of assignment, and report any suspected loss, theft or unauthorized disclosure immediately, and in any event within one business day of becoming aware of it.

## 8. Training and Awareness

8.1 New employees receive a trade secret briefing during their first week. Engineers, buyers, sales staff and anyone with access to Tier 1 material receive a longer session with practical examples taken from our own plants, such as what to do when a visitor asks for a photograph of a fixture or when a supplier's engineer asks detailed questions about a heat treatment cycle.

8.2 Every employee completes the annual awareness module. Completion is tracked by Human Resources, and managers receive a monthly list of overdue staff. Access to Tier 1 material is suspended for anyone whose training is more than thirty days overdue until the module is complete.

8.3 The Intellectual Property Counsel runs short refreshers each quarter on current topics, such as protecting information when attending trade shows, sharing screens on video calls and responding to unsolicited requests from outside parties.

## 9. Incident Reporting and Response

9.1 Any suspected unauthorized disclosure, loss or theft of trade secret information must be reported to the Intellectual Property Counsel and to Information Security at once. Reports may be made by telephone, by email or through the anonymous ethics line.

9.2 The Intellectual Property Counsel convenes a response team within one business day. The team determines what was affected, contains the incident, preserves evidence, and decides whether notice to customers, regulators or law enforcement is needed. Where misappropriation is suspected, outside litigation counsel may be engaged and legal holds issued.

9.3 After an incident is closed, the information owner and the Intellectual Property Counsel prepare a short lessons-learned note and recommend any changes to controls, training or this policy.

## 10. Exceptions

10.1 Exceptions to the handling requirements in section 6 may be granted in writing by the Chief Technology Officer for a defined period, subject to compensating controls. Each exception is recorded with the reason, the period and the controls, and is reviewed on expiry.

10.2 Exceptions to the external-sharing requirements in sections 3 to 5 follow the approval routes set out in the Supplier Engagement Policy, the Partner and Distributor Policy and the Legal Review and Approval Policy, and are not granted informally.

## 11. Enforcement

11.1 Violations of this policy may result in disciplinary action, up to and including termination of employment or engagement, subject to applicable law. Deliberate misappropriation of Keystone trade secrets may also lead to civil claims and referral to law enforcement.

11.2 Employees who report suspected violations in good faith are protected from retaliation. Any form of retaliation is itself a violation of the Code of Business Conduct.

11.3 Nothing in this policy prohibits an employee from reporting possible violations of law to a government agency or from making other disclosures that are protected under whistleblower laws.

## Appendix A. Examples of Classification

A.1 The following table gives examples to help information owners classify material. It is not exhaustive, and the Intellectual Property Counsel decides unclear cases.

| Item | Typical tier | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Heat treatment cycle for a titanium alloy bracket, including ramp rates and quench delay | Tier 1 | Took years to develop; cannot be seen from the finished part |
| Feeds-and-speeds library for nickel alloys | Tier 1 | Core machining know-how |
| Fixture designs for a proprietary part family | Tier 2 | Valuable but visible to anyone holding the fixture |
| Work instruction for a standard deburring operation | Tier 2 | Proprietary but not critical |
| Customer drawings furnished for quotation | Tier 2 or higher, as the customer requires | Held in trust |
| Plant floor layout | Tier 2 | Reveals flow and capacity |
| Cost model by part family | Tier 1 | Reveals margins and pricing method |
| Published catalog and brochure content | Public | Released by Corporate Communications |
| Employee safety training slides | Tier 3 | No technical sensitivity |
| Supplier scorecards | Tier 2 | Reveals sourcing strategy |

A.2 **Reasonable measures in practice.** Courts look at the totality of what a company does. They consider whether the owner restricted access, marked the information, trained staff, controlled visitors, used passwords and encryption, monitored use, and responded when something went wrong. Keystone is better protected if it can show that each of these measures was in place and applied consistently. A single missed control does not defeat a claim, but a pattern of ignoring rules does. For that reason, managers are asked to correct lapses when they see them and to record what was done.

A.3 **Common mistakes.** The following mistakes recur in companies similar to ours and should be avoided: leaving drawings on a conference room table after a meeting; discussing a process improvement in a restaurant after a trade show; sending a file to a personal email account to work on it at home; allowing a visitor to photograph a fixture because "it's only a bracket"; failing to remove access for a transferred engineer; and posting photographs of a new machine setup on social media.

A.4 **Reporting a concern.** If you are unsure whether something should be protected, treat it as protected and ask the Intellectual Property Counsel. If you see something that does not look right, such as a person you do not recognize in a restricted area, an unusual download or a colleague copying drawings to a personal drive, report it. You will not be penalized for a good-faith report that turns out to be a false alarm.
