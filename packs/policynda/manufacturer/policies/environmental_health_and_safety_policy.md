# Environmental Health and Safety Policy

**Keystone Precision Manufacturing, Inc.**
Policy owner: Director of Environmental Health and Safety
Policy approver: Chief Executive Officer
Effective date: January 2, 2025
Review cycle: annually
Document number: KPM-EH-001

---

## 1. Purpose and Commitment

1.1 Keystone Precision Manufacturing, Inc. ("Keystone") operates machining plants with high-speed spindles, heavy presses, furnaces, coolant systems, welding cells and overhead cranes. These are all hazards. Nothing we make is worth an injury, and no schedule is so tight that it justifies working unsafely. Our goal is simple: every person who comes to work at a Keystone facility goes home in the same condition in which they arrived.

1.2 This policy states Keystone's commitment to the health and safety of employees, contractors and visitors and to the protection of the environment. It sets minimum standards for all plants, assigns responsibilities and describes the programs through which we find and fix hazards. Plant-level procedures may be stricter than this policy but never less strict.

1.3 Keystone commits to comply with all applicable environmental, health and safety laws, including the Occupational Safety and Health Act and its regulations, the Clean Air Act, the Clean Water Act, the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act, the Emergency Planning and Community Right-to-Know Act, and the corresponding state laws of Ohio, Pennsylvania and Michigan and the Mexican federal and state laws applying to our Monterrey cell; to prevent injury, illness and pollution; to set targets and track them; and to improve continually.

## 2. Scope and Definitions

2.1 **Scope.** This policy applies to all Keystone employees, temporary workers, interns, contractors working on site and visitors, and to all facilities, including offices, warehouses and parking areas.

2.2 **Definitions.**

- **Recordable injury:** a work-related injury or illness that meets the criteria for recording under OSHA regulation 29 CFR 1904.
- **Total Recordable Incident Rate (TRIR):** the number of recordable injuries multiplied by 200,000 and divided by the total hours worked.
- **Near miss:** an event that could have resulted in injury, illness or damage but did not.
- **Lockout/tagout (LOTO):** the control of hazardous energy by isolating a machine from its energy sources and locking and tagging the isolation device before maintenance or servicing.
- **Hazardous material:** a substance that poses a physical or health hazard, as defined by the Hazard Communication Standard.
- **Safety data sheet (SDS):** the document that describes a chemical's hazards, handling and emergency measures.
- **Personal protective equipment (PPE):** equipment worn to minimize exposure to hazards, such as safety glasses, hearing protection, gloves and face shields.
- **Competent person:** a person who is capable of identifying hazards and has authority to take prompt corrective action.

## 3. Leadership and Responsibilities

3.1 **Chief Executive Officer.** Sets the commitment, provides resources and reviews safety performance quarterly.

3.2 **Director of EHS.** Owns this policy, leads the EHS team of plant specialists, interprets regulations, reports metrics and ensures audits and training happen.

3.3 **Plant managers.** Accountable for safety in their plants. They chair the plant safety committee, ensure that hazards are corrected, allocate resources, and take part in monthly walk-throughs.

3.4 **Supervisors.** Enforce safe work practices, perform daily start-of-shift safety talks, conduct job hazard analyses, investigate incidents and correct unsafe conditions at once or request help.

3.5 **Employees.** Follow safety rules, use required PPE, report hazards, injuries and near misses, and take part in training. Every employee has the right and responsibility to refuse work that they reasonably believe presents an imminent danger of death or serious injury and to stop another person's unsafe work.

3.6 **Safety committees.** Each plant has a joint management-employee safety committee that meets monthly, reviews incidents, inspects the facility and recommends improvements. At least half of the members are hourly employees.

## 4. Hazard Identification and Risk Control

4.1 **Job hazard analysis.** A job hazard analysis (JHA) is completed for each task that involves a significant hazard and for each new or changed process. The JHA lists the steps, the hazards, the controls and the PPE. Controls follow the hierarchy: eliminate, substitute, engineer, administer, and use PPE as the last resort.

4.2 **Workplace inspections.** Supervisors perform a daily walk-through of their areas. The plant safety committee conducts a monthly inspection. The EHS specialist performs a quarterly formal inspection covering guarding, housekeeping, electrical, fire protection, emergency egress, forklift operation, chemical storage and waste management. Deficiencies are entered in the action tracking system with an owner and due date.

4.3 **Management of change.** Changes to equipment, processes, materials or layouts are reviewed by EHS before implementation, so that new hazards are identified and controlled.

4.4 **Contractors.** Contractors working at Keystone sites must be pre-qualified for safety, including experience modification rate, injury history and written safety program. They attend an orientation before starting and follow the same site rules as employees. The Keystone sponsor is responsible for coordinating work that may affect others.

## 5. Machine Safety

5.1 **Guarding.** All moving parts, pinch points and rotating parts of machines must be guarded. Guards may not be removed or bypassed except for maintenance under lockout/tagout, and they must be replaced before the machine is restarted. Machines whose guards or interlocks are not working are tagged out of service.

5.2 **Lockout/tagout.** Authorized employees receive training and issue personal locks. Group lockout procedures are used for larger jobs. Each machine has a written energy control procedure that identifies energy sources, isolation points and verification steps. The procedure is audited annually.

5.3 **Robots and automated cells.** Employees who work on or near robots and automated cells receive specific training. Interlocked gates, light curtains and area scanners must be tested every shift and verified by the safety specialist every quarter.

5.4 **Presses and heavy equipment.** Press brakes and presses are fitted with two-hand controls or presence-sensing devices. Press operators receive certification. Overhead cranes and hoists are inspected before each shift and annually by a qualified inspector, and operators are trained on rigging and load handling.

5.5 **Powered industrial trucks.** Only trained and certified operators may drive forklifts, and they must complete a pre-use inspection. Pedestrian walkways are marked, and forklifts must stop at intersections and sound the horn at blind corners. Travel speed is limited to five miles per hour inside buildings.

## 6. Chemical and Hazardous Material Management

6.1 **Hazard communication.** Containers must be labeled. SDSs for every chemical in the workplace are available in the plant's electronic SDS system and on the shop floor. Employees are trained on hazards and protective measures before they use a new chemical, and whenever a new hazard is introduced.

6.2 **Coolants and cutting fluids.** Metalworking fluids are managed according to a program that includes concentration checks, bacterial monitoring, skimming, filtering and scheduled replacement. Mist collectors are installed on machines where exposure may exceed limits. Employees with skin reactions are referred to the occupational health nurse.

6.3 **Metals and dusts.** Operations that produce dust or fumes containing beryllium, hexavalent chromium, cobalt, lead or titanium require exposure assessment, local exhaust ventilation and respiratory protection as determined by the industrial hygienist. Fine metal powders and chips of reactive metals such as titanium and magnesium are collected in approved containers and stored away from ignition sources. Class D fire extinguishers are placed in relevant areas.

6.4 **Storage.** Flammable liquids are stored in approved cabinets, limited to quantities allowed by fire code. Incompatible chemicals are separated. Secondary containment is provided for bulk containers. Gas cylinders are secured upright and capped when not in use.

6.5 **Spill response.** Each department has spill kits. Employees trained as responders may clean up small spills. Large spills or spills reaching floor drains or outdoors are reported to the plant EHS specialist immediately, who decides on notification of the authorities under the reporting thresholds.

## 7. Occupational Health

7.1 **Noise.** Where noise levels equal or exceed 85 decibels as an eight-hour average, Keystone provides hearing protection, annual audiometric testing and training, and posts the area. Engineering controls are preferred to hearing protection where feasible.

7.2 **Respiratory protection.** Respirators are issued only after a medical evaluation and fit test. Employees must be clean-shaven where the respirator seals against the face. Fit tests are repeated annually.

7.3 **Ergonomics.** Supervisors and the EHS team look for tasks that require repetitive motion, awkward postures, heavy lifting or sustained force. Workstation changes, lifting aids and job rotation are used to reduce risk. Employees are encouraged to report discomfort early. Manual lifting above 50 pounds requires a mechanical aid or a team lift.

7.4 **Heat and cold.** Furnace, forge and welding areas have heat stress controls, including hydration, rest breaks and acclimatization for new or returning employees. When outdoor temperatures exceed 90 degrees Fahrenheit, supervisors review work schedules and provide additional breaks.

7.5 **Medical surveillance.** The occupational health nurse manages medical surveillance programs required by regulation or indicated by the exposure assessment. Medical records are kept separate from personnel files and are available to the employee on request.

## 8. Emergency Preparedness

8.1 **Emergency action plan.** Each facility has a written plan that covers evacuation routes and assembly areas, accounting for all personnel, alarm systems, roles, shelter in place for severe weather, and reporting emergencies. Fire drills are held at least twice a year, and tornado drills annually at the Ohio plants.

8.2 **Fire protection.** Fire extinguishers are inspected monthly and serviced annually. Sprinkler and alarm systems are tested as required by code. Hot work requires a permit, a fire watch and a check of the area thirty minutes after completion.

8.3 **First aid.** Each shift has at least three employees trained in first aid, CPR and use of automated external defibrillators. First aid kits and AEDs are located and inspected monthly. Serious injuries are treated by calling 911 first, and then notifying the supervisor.

8.4 **Crisis communication.** The plant manager acts as the incident commander for emergencies at the plant. Corporate Communications handles all communications with the media and the public. Families of injured employees are contacted by Human Resources.

## 9. Environmental Management

9.1 **Air.** Emission sources such as paint booths, degreasers, furnaces and welding are inventoried and permitted as required. Plants operate within permit limits, keep records of material usage and perform required reporting. Changes to sources are reviewed by EHS before implementation.

9.2 **Water.** Process wastewater from washers, coolant systems and plating lines is treated before discharge to publicly owned treatment works under the applicable permit. Stormwater is managed under a pollution prevention plan, with outdoor storage of materials protected from rain, regular sweeping, inspection and sampling.

9.3 **Waste.** Wastes are characterized, labeled, stored in designated areas and shipped by licensed transporters to permitted facilities. Hazardous waste accumulation areas are inspected weekly. Generator status is determined for each plant, and employees receive the training required for that status. Scrap metal and chips are recycled wherever possible, and the proceeds are tracked by plant.

9.4 **Energy and emissions.** Keystone aims to reduce energy use per unit produced by 20 percent from the 2020 baseline by 2030, through compressed air leak programs, high-efficiency motors, lighting upgrades, heat recovery on furnaces and shutdown of idle equipment. Scope 1 and 2 greenhouse gas emissions are inventoried annually.

9.5 **Reporting.** EHS prepares reports required by regulation, including the Toxics Release Inventory, tier II chemical inventory and greenhouse gas reporting where thresholds are exceeded. Reports are reviewed by the Director of EHS and the General Counsel before submission.

## 10. Incident Reporting and Investigation

10.1 **Immediate reporting.** Employees must report every injury, illness, near miss, spill, fire and property damage event to their supervisor immediately. The supervisor secures the scene, ensures medical treatment and notifies EHS within one hour of any serious event.

10.2 **Regulatory notification.** The Director of EHS notifies OSHA within eight hours of a work-related fatality and within 24 hours of an in-patient hospitalization, amputation or loss of an eye, as required by law.

10.3 **Investigation.** Supervisors begin an investigation immediately. For serious events and significant near misses, a team including EHS, a representative of employees and a manager completes a root cause analysis within seven days and recommends corrective actions. Findings are shared across plants.

10.4 **Recordkeeping.** EHS maintains OSHA logs, incident reports, training records, inspection results, exposure data and medical surveillance records for the period required by law, and at least thirty years for exposure and medical records where the regulation requires.

## 11. Training

11.1 All new employees receive safety orientation before beginning work, covering emergency procedures, hazard communication, PPE, lockout/tagout awareness, machine safety, ergonomics and reporting. Task-specific training is provided before an employee begins any job with a significant hazard.

11.2 Refresher training is provided annually, or as required by regulation, and when employees change jobs, when new equipment is introduced or after an incident indicating a training gap. Training records are kept by EHS and Human Resources.

## 12. Performance Measurement and Audits

12.1 Safety performance is measured by TRIR, days away, restricted or transferred rate, near miss reporting rate, completion of corrective actions, training completion and results of inspections. The corporate target for TRIR is below 1.5, and each plant sets an annual improvement goal.

12.2 Each plant is audited by the corporate EHS team every year. External consultants audit environmental compliance every three years. Audit findings are rated, assigned to owners and tracked to closure. The Director of EHS reports status to the Chief Executive Officer each quarter and to the Board annually.

## 13. Exceptions

13.1 A request to deviate from a requirement in this policy must be submitted to the Director of EHS in writing. A deviation is granted only if it provides equivalent protection, has a defined end date and is approved by the plant manager. No deviation may contravene law.

## 14. Enforcement

14.1 Compliance with this policy is a condition of employment. Employees who violate safety rules, disable safety devices, falsify records or retaliate against someone who raises a concern may face discipline up to and including termination. Supervisors who tolerate unsafe practices are also accountable.

14.2 Keystone does not discriminate against any employee who reports an injury, raises a safety concern or exercises rights under health and safety laws.

## Appendix A. Plant Notes and Quick Reference

A.1 **Dayton headquarters and machining plant.** The Dayton plant has 310 employees on three shifts. Its principal hazards are high-speed turning and milling, large presses in the forming bay, the heat treatment furnaces and the overhead cranes in the heavy fabrication area. The plant has a dedicated EHS specialist and an occupational health nurse on the day shift. The nearest hospital is a ten-minute drive, and the plant's emergency assembly points are in the east and west parking areas.

A.2 **Toledo machining plant.** The Toledo plant has 190 employees and runs two shifts. Its principal hazards are the five-axis cells, the automated pallet systems and the washing and deburring lines. The plant is a Large Quantity Generator of hazardous waste because of its spent solvents and filter media, and its EHS specialist keeps the manifest records, performs the weekly accumulation area inspections and supervises the annual emergency response training.

A.3 **Erie machining plant.** The Erie plant has 140 employees and produces medical device components in a clean machining area and a cleanroom for final cleaning and packaging. In addition to the standard program, the plant follows the gowning, cleanliness and chemical handling procedures required in the quality system. Employees working with passivation and cleaning chemicals use splash protection, eyewash stations are tested weekly, and neutralization tanks are inspected daily.

A.4 **Monterrey assembly cell.** The Monterrey cell has 65 employees. It follows the Mexican federal labor and safety standards (the NOM standards) as well as this policy, and wherever the two differ, the stricter requirement applies. Safety training is provided in Spanish, and signs and procedures are bilingual. Emergency services in Monterrey differ from those in the United States, and the plant manager reviews with local responders the plan for an emergency at the plant each year.

A.5 **Daily safety talk topics.** Supervisors rotate through the following topics during start-of-shift talks. Week one: eye and face protection. Week two: lockout/tagout. Week three: housekeeping and slip hazards. Week four: hand safety and cut resistance. Week five: forklift awareness. Week six: ergonomics and lifting. Week seven: chemical labels and safety data sheets. Week eight: fire extinguisher use and evacuation routes. Week nine: hearing protection. Week ten: machine guarding. After week ten the cycle begins again, with examples taken from recent events.

A.6 **Personal protective equipment matrix.** Safety glasses with side shields are required in all production areas, and tinted or face shields are used for grinding, welding and wire brushing. Hearing protection is required in posted areas. Cut-resistant gloves are used when handling sharp parts or chips, but gloves must not be worn when operating rotating equipment such as drills, lathes or mills, where they can be caught. Steel-toed safety shoes are required in production, warehouse and shipping areas, and Keystone provides an allowance of $125 per year toward their cost. Long hair must be tied back, and loose jewelry and clothing are not allowed near moving machinery.

A.7 **Safety recognition.** Each plant recognizes teams that achieve milestones such as 365 days without a recordable injury, completion of a significant hazard elimination project or an outstanding contribution by a safety committee. Recognition is based on leading indicators, such as hazard reports closed, rather than on injury counts alone, because tying rewards only to injury rates can discourage the reporting of injuries.

A.8 **Hazard report form.** Any employee can submit a hazard report using the card at the safety board, the mobile form, or a telephone call to the EHS specialist. The report asks for location, description of the hazard, potential consequence and any suggested correction. The supervisor acknowledges every report within one business day, the corrective action is assigned within three business days, and the result is posted on the safety board.
